One promoter, many genomes

Week 1about 3 hours

A research project of the Chronomic Medicine Group. You will take two small variants in the promoter of a heart gene, SCN5A, and ask what larger stretch of DNA they belong to – reading hundreds of complete human genomes, scoring the motifs the variants sit in, tracing them back through our evolution, and testing them in biobank-scale data on the heart.

The question

SCN5A makes Nav1.5, the sodium channel that starts each heartbeat. Its promoter – the switch that controls how much of the channel is made – carries two variants that our group has studied for years for a link to the circadian (daily) control of the heart: rs41310749 and rs41310239. They are known to travel together. What nobody has described is the larger haplotype – the whole block of DNA inherited as one piece – that they are part of.

Your project: use the long-read genomes of the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium and the 1000 Genomes Project to find and measure that block; work out which transcription-factor motifs the variants change (including the clock's E-boxes); trace the variants and the motifs through archaic humans and other primates; and ask what the block does, in a genome-wide study of a heart-rhythm disease and in gene-expression data.

As far as we know, nobody has described this haplotype before. The guide takes you through it in eleven stages, about one a week. Each stage teaches you the tools it needs – you do not need to have programmed before. By the end you will have used a Linux terminal, real bioinformatics programs, R, a genome browser and public databases, and you will have results of your own to write up.

The science: clocks, the heart and SCN5A

Week 1about 2 hours of reading

Before the first command, the biology. This page is the background you need; the reading list goes deeper, and the glossary explains the words.

A clock in every cell

Almost every cell in the body keeps time. A small set of clock genes switch each other on and off in a loop that takes about 24 hours: the proteins CLOCK and BMAL1 join together and switch on genes by binding a short piece of DNA called an E-box (its core letters are CACGTG, or more loosely CANNTG); among the genes they switch on are PER and CRY, whose proteins then switch CLOCK–BMAL1 back off. This loop runs in the heart as it does in the brain, and through it the clock shapes the day–night rhythm of heart rate, blood pressure and electrical activity. Heart attacks and dangerous arrhythmias are commoner at some times of day than others.

The heartbeat and SCN5A

Each heartbeat begins when sodium ions rush into heart-muscle cells through a channel in their surface. That channel is Nav1.5, and the gene that makes it is SCN5A, on human chromosome 3. How much Nav1.5 a cell makes depends on the gene's promoter – the stretch of DNA just before the gene where the machinery that reads genes assembles, and where transcription factors (including clock proteins) bind to turn the gene up or down.

When SCN5A makes too little working channel, the heart's electrical signal spreads too slowly. This causes conduction disease and Brugada syndrome – an inherited condition that can cause dangerous rhythms and sudden death, often at rest or during sleep, which is one reason the clock is of interest. SCN5A sits next to a related gene, SCN10A, and DNA between the two genes helps control SCN5A – the neighbourhood matters, which is part of why this project looks at a whole block of DNA, not just two letters.

Strand and coordinates – read this now

SCN5A is written on the minus strand of chromosome 3. That means the gene is read from higher chromosome positions to lower ones, so its promoter lies just above the gene body in coordinates. The start of the gene (its transcription start site, TSS) is near chr3:38,649,700 (GRCh38), and the promoter runs upward from there. Keep a note of this: it trips people up in stage 1.

The two variants

This project is built around two single-letter differences (SNPs) in the SCN5A promoter:

  • rs41310749 — chr3:38,651,526 (GRCh38), where most chromosomes have G and some have A. About 1,840 letters above the TSS.
  • rs41310239 — chr3:38,650,537, where most have A and some have G. About 850 letters above the TSS.

In 2012, Park and colleagues reported that these two SNPs are in strong linkage disequilibrium – their minor alleles travel together on the same chromosomes – and that, in families carrying a disease mutation elsewhere in SCN5A, people who also carried this two-SNP haplotype had more severe disease. They predicted, from the sequence, that the variants create binding sites for the nuclear receptor RXR and for Hes1 (a relative of the clock's bHLH proteins). Our group has studied these and nearby promoter motifs for a link to the clock's control of the heart.

The gap in the story

Two SNPs that travel together are the tip of something larger: a whole haplotype block of variants inherited as one piece. Nobody has described that block for these two SNPs – how far it reaches, which other variants and regulatory elements it contains, how common it is in different populations, where it came from in our evolution, or what it does to the heart at the scale of hundreds of thousands of people. Long-read pangenomes and large biobanks can now answer these questions. That is your project.

What you will test

  1. The block. How far does the haplotype of rs41310749 and rs41310239 extend, and what is in it? (Stages 3–7)
  2. The motifs. Do the variants change transcription-factor binding sites – in particular clock E-boxes? (Stages 2 and 8)
  3. The history. How old is the haplotype? Is it in Neanderthals and Denisovans? Are the variant sites and the E-boxes conserved across primates? (Stage 9)
  4. The consequence. Is the haplotype associated with heart disease, and does it change how much SCN5A is made? (Stage 10)

None of these has a settled answer in the literature for this particular haplotype – which is what makes it a research project and not an exercise. Keep that in mind: the Check boxes tell you when your numbers are right, but what they mean is for you to argue.

Glossary

referencecome back when a word is new

Plain-language definitions of the words this project uses. They are rough and practical, not textbook-exact; the reading list is where to go deeper.

Allele
One of the versions of a position in the genome. rs41310749 has alleles G and A.
SNP / SNV
A single-letter difference between genomes (single-nucleotide polymorphism / variant). Both the project's variants are SNPs.
Reference allele / alternate allele
The letter in the reference genome (REF), and any other letter seen (ALT). "Alternate" does not mean "bad" or "rare".
Ancestral / derived allele
The older allele (the one our ancestors had, usually read from other primates) and the newer one that arose by mutation.
Minor allele; allele frequency
The less common allele at a position, and how often an allele is seen (as a fraction of all chromosomes).
Promoter
The stretch of DNA just before a gene where the machinery that reads it assembles and transcription factors bind to turn it up or down.
Transcription factor; motif; E-box
A protein that binds DNA to control a gene; the short DNA pattern it recognises (a motif); the clock proteins' motif is the E-box, core CACGTG.
TSS
Transcription start site: where reading of the gene begins. Because SCN5A is on the minus strand, its promoter is at higher coordinates than the TSS.
Strand
DNA has two strands; a gene is read from one of them. SCN5A is read from the minus strand, so "upstream" means higher chromosome positions.
Haplotype
The particular combination of alleles on one chromosome, inherited together.
Phased
Genotypes written so you know which allele is on which of a person's two chromosomes (0|1, with a bar) rather than just the pair (0/1). You need phasing to see haplotypes.
Linkage disequilibrium (LD)
When two variants are found together more often than chance, because they sit near each other on the same haplotypes. Measured by r² (0–1) and D′ (0–1).
Haplotype block
A run of DNA whose variants are in strong LD and are inherited as one piece. Finding the block of the two variants is the heart of this project.
Tag SNP
A variant whose allele predicts another variant's allele (high r²), so measuring one measures the other.
VCF
Variant Call Format: the standard text file of variants and the genotype of each person. Read it with bcftools.
Pangenome
A reference made from many complete genomes at once (here, long-read assemblies), so it can show sequence that a single reference and short reads miss.
GWAS
Genome-wide association study: testing millions of variants, one at a time, for a link to a trait or disease, across many people.
Summary statistics
A GWAS's full results: for each variant, the effect and its P-value. Often public.
eQTL
A variant associated with how much a gene is expressed (how much RNA is made). An "expression quantitative trait locus".
Conservation
How little a piece of DNA has changed across species. Conserved DNA is often (not always) functional.
Brugada syndrome
An inherited heart-rhythm disorder, often from reduced SCN5A/Nav1.5 function, that can cause dangerous rhythms, often at rest or in sleep.
superpopulation
The broad ancestry groups of the 1000 Genomes Project: AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR, SAS. Useful for comparing allele frequencies; not the same as "race".

Get this straight now and the rest of the project makes sense.

Stage 2 · The promoter and its motifs

Weeks 2–3about 5 hours

The two variants sit in the SCN5A promoter. Before you ask what haplotype they belong to, get to know the promoter itself: pull out its sequence, measure how GC-rich it is, and find the clock's E-boxes – and see exactly where the two variants fall among them. You will come back to this in stage 8.

You will learn
  • to cut a piece of sequence out of a genome and work with it as text
  • GC content and CpG, and why promoters are often GC-rich
  • regular expressions, and finding a motif with grep
  • what an E-box is, and where the clock proteins bind
E-boxes and the clock

The clock proteins CLOCK and BMAL1 switch genes on by binding a short DNA motif, the E-box. Its strongest form is the six letters CACGTG (a palindrome – the same read on either strand); more loosely, any CANNTG (where N is any letter) can be an E-box, bound by one or another member of the bHLH family of proteins. If the SCN5A promoter is under clock control, it should contain E-boxes – and a variant that falls in or near one could change how the clock reads the gene. That is the thread this project follows.

1. Cut out the promoter

The TSS is near chr3:38,649,687 and the promoter runs upward from there. Take the 2.2 kb from the TSS to just above the second variant, and save it as a FASTA file and as plain letters:

cd ~/project
samtools faidx /data/examples/scn5a/reference/GRCh38_chr3_SCN5A.fa.gz chr3:38649687-38651900 > results/promoter.fa
grep -v "^>" results/promoter.fa | tr -d '\n' > results/promoter.txt
wc -c results/promoter.txt

grep -v "^>" drops the FASTA header line, and tr -d '\n' removes the line breaks, leaving one long string of letters.

Check

The promoter piece is 2,214 letters long.

2. How GC-rich is it?

Count the G and C letters. grep -o prints each match on its own line, and wc -l counts them:

GC=$(grep -o -E "[GC]" results/promoter.txt | wc -l)
AT=$(grep -o -E "[AT]" results/promoter.txt | wc -l)
awk -v gc=$GC -v at=$AT 'BEGIN{ printf "GC: %d   AT: %d   GC%% = %.1f\n", gc, at, 100*gc/(gc+at) }'
Check

About 56–57% GC – much more than the genome's ~41% average. GC-rich, CpG-dense stretches like this are typical of promoters, and often fall in a CpG island. Hold that thought: one of the variants (rs41310239) sits in an especially C/G-rich patch.

3. Find the E-boxes

The canonical E-box is CACGTG. Find every one, with its position, using grep:

grep -o CACGTG results/promoter.txt | wc -l
grep -b -o CACGTG results/promoter.txt

-b prints the 0-based offset of each match within the piece. Add 38,649,687 to an offset to get the chromosome position.

Check

Three canonical E-boxes (CACGTG), at offsets 147, 446 and 1748 – that is chr3:38,649,834, 38,650,133 and 38,651,435 (147, 446 and 1,748 bases from the TSS). The last of these is just 91 bases from rs41310749 (38,651,526).

The looser E-box CANNTG is much commoner. The dot in a regular expression means "any letter":

grep -o -E "CA..TG" results/promoter.txt | wc -l
grep -o -E "CA..TG" results/promoter.txt | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn
Check

About 13 matches of CANNTG on this strand (there are more on the other strand). The promoter is dense with E-box-like motifs – it is the kind of promoter the clock could read.

Linkage disequilibrium

Every mutation arises on one chromosome, beside the variants that chromosome already carries. Recombination slowly shuffles them, but variants close together are rarely separated and stay together on the same haplotypes for many generations. Variants found together more often than chance are in linkage disequilibrium (LD). Two measures: r² (the squared correlation, 0–1) is high only when two variants have similar frequencies and predict each other; D′ (0–1) is high whenever one allele is found almost only with one allele of the other, even if their frequencies differ. A haplotype block is a run of variants in high mutual LD.

From counts to a score

JASPAR stores, for each transcription factor, how often each base appears at each position of its binding site – a position frequency matrix (PFM). Turn each count into a log-odds score (how much more likely that base is here than by chance) and you have a PWM: slide it along a sequence, add up the scores, and the best-scoring window is the factor's likely binding site. Score the window with the reference base and again with the alternate: the difference says whether the variant strengthens or weakens the site. It is a prediction, not proof – but a good place to start.

2. Read the matrices and score

These functions read the JASPAR file, build a PWM, and score the best window of a sequence (on either strand):

read_jaspar <- function(path) {
  L <- readLines(path); mats <- list(); name <- NULL; rows <- list(); mid <- NULL
  for (ln in L) {
    if (startsWith(ln, ">")) {
      if (!is.null(name)) mats[[mid]] <- list(name = name, pfm = do.call(rbind, rows))
      p <- strsplit(sub("^>", "", ln), "\t")[[1]]; mid <- p[1]; name <- p[2]; rows <- list()
    } else if (nchar(trimws(ln)) > 0) {
      rows[[length(rows) + 1]] <- as.numeric(regmatches(ln, gregexpr("[0-9.]+", ln))[[1]])
    }
  }
  if (!is.null(name)) mats[[mid]] <- list(name = name, pfm = do.call(rbind, rows))
  mats
}
pwm_lods <- function(pfm, pseudo = 0.8, bg = 0.25)      # rows A,C,G,T; columns = positions
  t(apply(pfm, 2, function(col) { tot <- sum(col) + 4 * pseudo; log2(((col + pseudo) / tot) / bg) }))
revcomp <- function(s) { m <- c(A="T",T="A",G="C",C="G",N="N"); paste(m[rev(strsplit(s,"")[[1]])], collapse="") }
score_seq <- function(lods, s) {
  Lw <- nrow(lods); idx <- c(A=1,C=2,G=3,T=4); best <- -1e9
  for (seq in c(s, revcomp(s))) for (i in 1:(nchar(seq)-Lw+1)) {
    chs <- strsplit(substring(seq,i,i+Lw-1),"")[[1]]
    if (!any(chs=="N")) { sc <- sum(lods[cbind(1:Lw, idx[chs])]); if (sc>best) best <- sc }
  }
  best
}

Now a function that scores a variant's reference and alternate allele against one matrix, in its own sequence context:

score_variant <- function(ctx_file, ref, alt, matrix_id) {
  flank <- toupper(readLines(ctx_file)[1])               # 41 bases: 20 + allele + 20
  left <- substr(flank, 1, 20); right <- substr(flank, 22, 41)
  lods <- pwm_lods(mats[[matrix_id]]$pfm); Lw <- nrow(lods)
  wl <- substr(left, 21 - (Lw - 1), 20); wr <- substr(right, 1, Lw - 1)   # window must overlap the variant
  mx <- sum(apply(lods, 1, max))                          # the best possible score
  ref_s <- score_seq(lods, paste0(wl, ref, wr)); alt_s <- score_seq(lods, paste0(wl, alt, wr))
  data.frame(TF = mats[[matrix_id]]$name, ref_rel = round(ref_s/mx, 3), alt_rel = round(alt_s/mx, 3),
             delta = round(alt_s - ref_s, 2))
}

VCF files and bcftools

The variant call format (VCF) is how genome projects share the differences between genomes and a reference. bcftools reads, filters, reshapes and combines VCF files; with bgzip and tabix it keeps them compressed and indexed.

The format

A VCF file is text, with a header and one line per variant:

##fileformat=VCFv4.2
##INFO=<ID=AC,Number=A,Type=Integer,Description="Total number of alternate alleles in called genotypes">
##FORMAT=<ID=GT,Number=1,Type=String,Description="Genotype">
#CHROM  POS      ID  REF  ALT  QUAL  FILTER  INFO          FORMAT  HG00097  HG00099
chr3    38651526 rs41310749 G A 60 .       AC=56;AN=462  GT      0|0      1|0
ColumnHolds
CHROM, POSthe sequence and the position of the first letter of REF, counted from 1
IDthe variant's name (an rsID from dbSNP), or .
REF, ALTthe reference allele, and the other alleles separated by commas (a variant with several ALT alleles is multi-allelic). An insertion or deletion includes the letter before it: TC → T deletes a C.
QUAL, FILTERthe quality of the call, and the filters it passed (PASS) or failed
INFOKEY=value pairs about the variant, described in the header: AC allele counts, AN number of alleles, AF frequency …
FORMAT, sampleswhat each sample's column holds (GT, and often depth DP, quality GQ …), then one column per sample

Genotypes: 0 is REF, 1 the first ALT allele, 2 the second, . unknown. 0/1 is unphased (one of each, on unknown chromosomes); 0|1 is phased: REF on the first haplotype, ALT on the second. A haploid genotype has one allele (1).

A VCF is usually compressed with bgzip (.vcf.gz) and indexed with tabix -p vcf or bcftools index -t (.tbi): the index lets programs jump to a region. BCF is a binary form of the same thing.

Look at a VCF

V=/data/examples/scn5a/hprc/hprc_scn5a_promoter.vcf.gz
bcftools view -h $V | tail -3 | cut -c 1-120       # the end of the header
bcftools query -l $V | head -3                      # the samples
bcftools view -H $V | cut -f 1-8 | head -3          # the variants, without the header
bcftools view -H $V | wc -l                         # how many

bcftools view -H -v indels /data/examples/scn5a/hprc/hprc_scn5a_promoter.vcf.gz | cut -f 1-5 | cut -c 1-80
bcftools view -s HG00099 -r chr3:38651526 /data/examples/scn5a/hprc/hprc_scn5a_promoter.vcf.gz | tail -1

Expressions can use the columns (POS, QUAL, ID, REF, ALT), INFO fields (AC, INFO/AF), TYPE="snp", and the genotypes (GT="het", GT="AA"), joined with && and ||: -i 'POS==38651526 || POS==38650537'.

bcftools query: turn a VCF into a table

-f gives the layout: %CHROM, %POS, %ID, %REF, %ALT, %QUAL, %INFO/AC (or just %AC), \t a tab, \n a new line. What is in [ ] is repeated for each sample: [%GT], [%SAMPLE]. -H adds a header line; -l lists the samples.

bcftools query -f '%POS\t%REF\t%ALT\t%AC\n' /data/examples/scn5a/hprc/hprc_scn5a_promoter.vcf.gz | head -3
bcftools query -r chr3:38651526 -f '[%SAMPLE\t%GT\n]' /data/examples/scn5a/hprc/hprc_scn5a_promoter.vcf.gz | head -3
bcftools query -r chr3:38651526 -f '[%GT\n]' /data/examples/scn5a/hprc/hprc_scn5a_promoter.vcf.gz | tr '|' '\n' | sort | uniq -c

The last line counts alleles: it splits each phased genotype at its bar.

bcftools annotate: add or remove information

bcftools annotate -a /data/examples/scn5a/reference/dbsnp157_scn5a_2Mb.vcf.gz -c ID -Oz -o ~/rsid.vcf.gz /data/examples/scn5a/hprc/hprc_scn5a_promoter.vcf.gz
bcftools view -H ~/rsid.vcf.gz | cut -f 1-5 | head -3
bcftools annotate -x INFO -Oz -o ~/noinfo.vcf.gz ~/rsid.vcf.gz

-a names a file to copy from, -c the columns to copy (here the ID, matched by position and alleles); -x removes fields (-x INFO/AF, -x ID, -x INFO).

bcftools consensus: a sequence with the variants put in

Applies the variants of one sample's haplotype to a reference sequence. -f is the reference (FASTA), -s the sample, -H 1 or -H 2 the haplotype of a phased VCF. The reference can be a region cut out with samtools faidx, named chr1:START-END: only the variants in it are applied.

samtools faidx /data/examples/scn5a/reference/GRCh38_chr3_SCN5A.fa.gz chr3:38651400-38651700 > ~/region.fa
bcftools consensus -f ~/region.fa -s HG00099 -H 1 /data/examples/scn5a/hprc/hprc_scn5a_promoter.vcf.gz | seqtk comp | cut -f 1,2

Other commands

  • bcftools norm -m -any splits multi-allelic variants into one line per ALT allele (-m +any joins them); with -f reference.fa it also left-aligns indels.
  • bcftools stats file.vcf.gz counts variants by type, and more.
  • bcftools isec finds the variants two files share, or do not; bcftools merge joins the samples of several files; bcftools concat joins files of different regions.
  • bcftools index -t file.vcf.gz (or tabix -p vcf) makes the index.
  • For genotypes of thousands of people, and association tests, see PLINK 2.

Traps

  • Chromosome names: chr1 in some files, 1 in others. A region that does not match the file's names finds nothing.
  • The genome build: positions in GRCh37 and GRCh38 differ. Check the header (##reference, ##contig lengths), and join files from different builds by rsID, or lift the positions over.
  • Overlapping variants: -r returns every variant that overlaps the region, including a long deletion that starts before it; add -i 'POS==…' for the one that starts there.
  • Missing genotypes (.) are not REF. Count them, and decide what to do with them.

Genotypes and association: PLINK 2

PLINK works with the genotypes of many people at once – thousands of people, millions of variants. It checks their quality, counts allele frequencies, measures linkage disequilibrium and relatedness, and tests each variant for association with a trait: a genome-wide association study, or GWAS. Every PLINK command has the same shape: the data (--vcf, --pfile, --bfile), filters, what to compute, and --out, the name of the files it writes.

The examples work in a folder of your own:

The data, and PLINK's own files

/data/examples/plink2/1kg-phase3-chr21.vcf.gz holds the genotypes of the 2,504 people of the 1000 Genomes Project (Phase 3) at the first 600 variants of chromosome 21 – real public data, and the test file of PLINK 2 itself. A VCF file has a column for each person:

zcat /data/examples/plink2/1kg-phase3-chr21.vcf.gz | grep -v '^##' | head -3 | cut -f 1-5,10-12
plink2 --vcf /data/examples/plink2/1kg-phase3-chr21.vcf.gz --make-pgen --out chr21
ls chr21.*

A genotype such as 0|1 gives the alleles of the two copies of the chromosome – 0 the reference allele (REF), 1 the alternative (ALT); | means phased: which copy carries which is known. --make-pgen writes PLINK 2's own files: chr21.pgen, the genotypes, packed tightly; chr21.pvar, the variants (the first columns of the VCF); chr21.psam, the people; and chr21.log, a record of what was done. --pfile chr21 reads the three back – much quicker than the VCF for every new command. PLINK 1's files, .bed, .bim and .fam, are still common: --make-bed writes them and --bfile reads them.

The log, and what PLINK prints, begin with the options in effect: besides yours, --threads 1 and --memory 640 require, which the terminal adds (see the end of this page).

Quality control: frequencies, missing genotypes, Hardy–Weinberg

plink2 --pfile chr21 --freq --missing --hardy --out qc
head -4 qc.afreq
awk 'NR > 1 && ($5 < 0.01 || $5 > 0.99)' qc.afreq | wc -l
tail -n +2 qc.hardy | sort -g -k10,10 | head -4

qc.afreq has the frequency of each ALT allele and the number of alleles counted – 5,008, two for each person. Most variants are rare: for 566 of the 600 the less common allele has a frequency below 1%, and hundreds were seen only once among the 5,008 chromosomes (a frequency of 0.0002). qc.smiss and qc.vmiss count the missing genotypes of each person and of each variant: none here, as the 1000 Genomes release gives every genotype – in data of your own, a person or a variant with many missing genotypes is a warning.

qc.hardy holds the Hardy–Weinberg exact test: are there as many heterozygotes as the allele frequency predicts? O(HET_A1) is the share of heterozygotes observed, E(HET_A1) the share expected, P the p-value. A few variants are very far from it, always with too many heterozygotes – 0.88 where 0.50 is expected. These variants lie on the short arm of chromosome 21, near its centromere, among repeated sequences – most likely, reads from two copies of a repeat were aligned to one place, and the difference between the copies looks like a heterozygous genotype in almost everyone. Such errors are what the Hardy–Weinberg filter is for.

Filters

plink2 --pfile chr21 --maf 0.01 --hwe 1e-6 --make-pgen --out chr21_qc

Filters act before anything else. --maf 0.01 keeps the variants whose minor allele frequency is 1% or more; --hwe 1e-6 removes those with a Hardy–Weinberg p-value below 10−6; --geno 0.02 would remove variants with more than 2% of their genotypes missing, and --mind 0.02 such people. The log says what each did: 25 variants removed by --hwe (all with too many heterozygotes), 566 by --maf, 9 remaining. --keep FILE and --remove FILE choose people by their IDs, --extract FILE and --exclude FILE variants, --chr with --from-bp and --to-bp a region.

Linkage disequilibrium

plink2 --pfile chr21_qc --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2 --out ld
cat ld.prune.in
plink2 --pfile chr21_qc --r2-unphased --ld-window-r2 0.2 --out ld
cat ld.vcor

Variants close to each other are inherited together, so their genotypes are correlated: linkage disequilibrium, measured as r2. --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2 moves along the chromosome in windows of 50 variants, 5 at a time, and of every pair with r2 above 0.2 keeps one: ld.prune.in lists the variants kept (6 of the 9), ld.prune.out the others. Analyses that expect independent variants – principal components, relatedness – use the pruned set (--extract ld.prune.in). --r2-unphased lists the pairs: rs559962037 and rs556687069, 229 bases apart, have r2 = 0.96 – they are nearly always inherited together.

Association: does a variant go with a trait?

The 1000 Genomes data has no trait to study, so these examples use synthetic data made for PLINK's own tests: 603 invented people, 30 variants (v1 … v30) and three phenotypes – BINARY (case 2 or control 1) and QUANT (a number), both drawn at random, and FIRTH, which makes every carrier of v1's ALT allele a case – with two covariates, C1 and C2 (in a real study: age, sex, principal components).

D=/data/examples/plink2
head -3 $D/synthetic_phenotypes.tsv
plink2 --vcf $D/synthetic_glm.vcf --pheno $D/synthetic_phenotypes.tsv --covar $D/synthetic_covariates.tsv --glm hide-covar --out assoc
ls assoc.*
cut -f 3,9,11-16 assoc.QUANT.glm.linear | head -5

--glm tests every variant with every phenotype: linear regression for a quantitative trait (assoc.QUANT.glm.linear), logistic regression for cases and controls (.glm.logistic.hybrid), with the covariates in each model (hide-covar: their own lines are left out). A line gives the variant, the allele tested (A1) and its frequency, the number of people, the effect – BETA, the change of the trait for each copy of A1, or OR, the odds ratio – its standard error, the test statistic and the p-value. QUANT and BINARY are random, so their p-values are spread between 0 and 1: of 30 tests, one or two fall below 0.05 by chance (here one for QUANT, none for BINARY). That is why a study is judged against the number of its tests – 0.05/30 here, and 5×10−8 for the million tests of a genome-wide study.

sort -g -k16,16 assoc.FIRTH.glm.logistic.hybrid | cut -f 3,10,13,16,17 | head -4

For FIRTH, v1 stands out. Every carrier of its ALT allele is a case – complete separation, for which ordinary logistic regression finds no odds ratio: it grows without end. PLINK then uses Firth's regression (FIRTH? Y), and still marks the result UNFINISHED: the odds ratio is in effect infinite. ERRCODE says when a result is not to be trusted as it stands. No other variant passes 0.05/30 = 0.0017. The p-values as a picture, in Python (a small "Manhattan plot"):

Scores

head -3 /data/examples/plink2/synthetic_scores.tsv
plink2 --pfile chr21 --score /data/examples/plink2/synthetic_scores.tsv 1 2 3 header-read cols=+scoresums --out score
head -4 score.sscore

A score adds up, for each person, the copies of chosen alleles, each multiplied by a weight. --score FILE 1 2 3 takes the variant, the allele and the weight from columns 1, 2 and 3 of the file (header-read: its first line is a header). score.sscore has, for each person, the alleles counted (ALLELE_CT, two for each of the 20 variants), how many of them were the named allele, and the score – the average over the alleles (WEIGHT_AVG) and, with cols=+scoresums, the sum (WEIGHT_SUM). A polygenic risk score is made this way, with the effect sizes of a published GWAS as the weights. The weights of synthetic_scores.tsv are made up: these scores mean nothing.

More, and what is not here

  • --export vcf (vcf bgz: compressed) writes a VCF file again, --make-bed PLINK 1's files, and --export A a table of 0, 1 or 2 copies for each person and variant, to read in Python or R.
  • --make-king-table estimates the kinship of each pair of people (KING), and --king-cutoff removes one of each related pair. It needs thousands of common variants: on 600 variants, most of them rare, its numbers are noise. /data/examples/plink2/keep48.samples.txt lists 48 of the people, for --keep.
  • plink2 --help --glm explains an option; plink2 alone lists the main ones. The manual is at cog-genomics.org/plink/2.0.
  • Not in this build: --pca (principal components, which correct an association study for ancestry) and --pheno-svd need the LAPACK library, which this WebAssembly build of PLINK 2 does not have – PLINK says so and stops. --script and --rerun (options read from a file) are not available either; plink2 $(cat FILE) reads options from a file.

Scripts and pipelines

Commands that work once belong in a script – so that they run again tomorrow, on new data, and show exactly what was done.

The examples work in a folder of your own:

A bash script

Write the commands into a file – in the Files tab, or with a here-document as below – and run it with bash:

cat > count_reads.sh << 'EOF'
#!/usr/bin/env bash
set -euo pipefail          # stop at the first error
for f in "$@"; do
  n=$(( $(wc -l < "$f") / 4 ))
  echo -e "$(basename "$f")\t$n"
done
EOF
bash count_reads.sh /data/examples/reads/*.fastq

"$@" are the file names given to the script; set -euo pipefail makes it stop at the first command that fails, instead of carrying on with half a result. After chmod +x count_reads.sh it also starts as ./count_reads.sh.

Asking from code

Each card uses a REST API: a web address goes in, data comes out – JSON for most, FASTA or text for sequences. The same works in a notebook:

import requests

r = requests.get("https://rest.ensembl.org/lookup/symbol/homo_sapiens/SCN5A",
                 headers={"Content-Type": "application/json"})
r.raise_for_status()          # stop here if the service said no
gene = r.json()
gene["id"], gene["seq_region_name"], gene["start"], gene["end"]
library(jsonlite)
gene <- fromJSON("https://rest.ensembl.org/lookup/symbol/homo_sapiens/SCN5A?content-type=application/json")
c(gene$id, gene$seq_region_name, gene$start, gene$end)

In the browser, R can only make GET requests (a URL); for services that need a POST request – BLAST, VEP with positions, the PDB search – use Python. More in the recipe Databases from code.

Good practice

  • Databases change. New releases add, correct and remove entries; a query can give another answer next month. Note the date – the notebook of your queries does – and, for positions on a genome, the assembly: GRCh38 (hg38) and GRCh37 (hg19) number the same base differently.
  • Annotations are predictions. VEP calls rs4244285 synonymous – yet it is CYP2C19*2, a loss-of-function allele that changes the splicing of the gene (see Genes, genomes and variants). Look at the clinical significance and the literature before you conclude.
  • The services are shared by scientists everywhere. Ask for what you need, not thousands of queries in a quick loop: Ensembl allows about 15 requests a second, NCBI 3. A request that gets no answer at all is sent again (twice) by the tab; for code, see the recipe.
  • When a query fails, the message says why: Not found (404) – check the identifier; did not accept the query (400) – check what you typed; slow down (429) – wait a minute; not answering (5xx) or no answer that the page could read – the service is busy or down: try again later.

Only what you type into a form is sent to the service (for BLAST, also your e-mail address). These are public services that keep logs: never type or paste personal or patient data into them.

UniProt

UniProtKB, the UniProt Knowledgebase, is the reference collection of protein sequences and of what is known about each protein. The UniProt card shows its entries laid out like the pages of uniprot.org, from live data.

Reviewed and unreviewed

UniProtKB has two parts. Swiss-Prot (★ Reviewed) entries are read and written by expert curators, from the literature; there are about 575,000 of them. TrEMBL (Unreviewed) entries – about 150 million – are annotated automatically, mostly from genome sequences (numbers of release 2026_03). When a reviewed entry exists, use it.

Each entry has an accession (P04637), which never changes and is the one to cite, and an entry name (P53_HUMAN). Its annotation score (1 to 5) says how much is known about it, not how correct it is.

Search

AlphaFold DB and InterPro

Two cards for what a protein's sequence says about it: AlphaFold DB has a predicted 3D structure for almost every protein in UniProt; InterPro finds its families, domains and important sites.

AlphaFold DB

AlphaFold predicts a protein's structure from its sequence. AlphaFold DB (EMBL-EBI and Google DeepMind) holds the predictions for over 200 million UniProt proteins. Type a UniProt accession and Search:

  • The table names the model (AF-P69905-F1), the protein and its organism, and the mean pLDDT.
  • The picture is the predicted aligned error (PAE).
  • Show in the 3D viewer loads the model, coloured by confidence; Save the model (.pdb) to my files keeps it in ~/structures.

How far to trust a model

pLDDTColourMeaning
above 90dark bluevery high confidence: side chains placed well too
70 – 90light blueconfident: the backbone is right
50 – 70yellowlow confidence
below 50orangevery low: often a region with no fixed structure (disordered), not a real shape

pLDDT is a confidence for each residue, about its own surroundings. It says nothing about how the domains lie relative to each other: that is what the PAE plot shows. Its colour in column x (the scored residue, along the top) and row y (the aligned residue, down the side) is the error AlphaFold expects in the position of residue x when the model is lined up on residue y: dark is a small error. Dark squares along the diagonal are domains placed confidently; light areas between them mean their arrangement is uncertain.

In the 3D viewer the model's panel has the pLDDT profile (click it to go to a residue), the PAE plot (drag a box over it: the two stretches of residues are selected, with the mean expected error between them), Hide pLDDT < 50, and Superpose onto… an experimental structure. A model is a prediction: where an experimental structure exists, compare them.

Genes, genomes and variants

Four cards for genes, genomes and sequencing data: Ensembl, NCBI, the UCSC Genome Browser and the European Nucleotide Archive.

Ensembl

Ensembl (EMBL-EBI) annotates the genomes of vertebrates and model organisms: genes, transcripts, proteins, variants, and how genes of different species are related. The card asks its REST API, which serves the latest release – for human, on the GRCh38 assembly.

Gene
A species and a gene symbol (SCN5A) or Ensembl gene ID (ENSG…). The answer gives the gene's ID, type (biotype), position and strand, and a table of its transcripts – which is the canonical one, how many exons, how long the protein is – with buttons that save each transcript's cDNA, CDS or protein sequence. Save the gene sequence (FASTA); for human genes, Show in the Genome browser (GRCh38).
Sequence
The sequence of an Ensembl gene, transcript or protein ID. genomic: the whole region, introns included (with extra bases on each side if you ask); cDNA: the exons of a transcript, untranslated regions included; CDS: from the start codon to the stop codon; protein.
Variant effect (VEP)
The Variant Effect Predictor: an rs ID (rs4244285), an HGVS description (ENST00000646891.2:c.1799T>A), or a chromosome, position, reference and alternative base on GRCh38 (10 94781859 G A). The answer gives the most severe consequence; the known variants at that position, with their clinical significance; allele frequencies (1000 Genomes, gnomAD); and for each transcript the consequence, its impact (HIGH, MODERATE, LOW, MODIFIER), the amino-acid change and the SIFT and PolyPhen predictions. A consequence is worked out from the position and the transcripts alone: rs4244285 comes out as synonymous (Pro227Pro), yet it is CYP2C19*2, the commonest loss-of-function allele of the gene – the base change makes a new splice site, and the mRNA is spliced wrongly. Read the clinical significance and the papers too.
Orthologues
The genes in another species – or in all of them – that descend from one gene of their last common ancestor: orthologues are separated by a speciation event (paralogues by a duplication within a genome).
Genes in a region
Every gene between two positions of a chromosome (at most 5 Mb).
GRCh38 and GRCh37

The same base has different positions on the two human assemblies: rs41310749 is at chr3:38,651,526 on GRCh38 and chr3:38,693,017 on GRCh37 (hg19). Many older papers, and some GWAS files, use GRCh37. In code, Ensembl's GRCh37 server is https://grch37.rest.ensembl.org; the UCSC card has both.

UCSC Genome Browser

The DNA of any region of a genome assembly: human hg38 (GRCh38) and hg19 (GRCh37), mouse, zebrafish, fly, worm and yeast. Write the region as chr3:38650400-38651600, counting from 1 as genome browsers do; at most 1 Mb. Bases in lower case are repeats (the sequence is soft-masked); upper and lower case are the same bases. For hg38 and hg19, Show this region in the Genome browser.

The UCSC API itself counts from 0 (start=7829833 for position 7,829,834); the code that Send to notebook writes takes care of it.

European Nucleotide Archive

ENA (EMBL-EBI) – with its partners NCBI's SRA and DDBJ – keeps the raw data of published sequencing experiments. Sequencing runs takes a study (PRJEB37886), sample or run (SRR098401) accession, or a query in ENA's language (tax_eq(9606) AND library_strategy="AMPLICON"), and lists the runs: organism, instrument, library strategy and layout, number of reads, and the FASTQ files. Click a file to download it into ~/reads.

Mind the size

A human genome or exome run is many gigabytes (the two FASTQ files of SRR098401 are 7.5 and 7.7 GB): far more than fits in a browser. Small runs – of viruses, bacteria, amplicons – are a few megabytes to a few hundred. The recipe Databases from code shows how to take just the first reads of a large file.

Sequence by accession gives any ENA/GenBank sequence as FASTA (MN908947.3 is the first SARS-CoV-2 genome).

BLAST

BLAST finds the sequences in a database that are similar to yours – the first thing to do with an unknown sequence. The BLAST card sends the search to EMBL-EBI, where it runs for seconds to a few minutes, and shows the hits.

A search

  1. Program – which depends on what your sequence is and what you search:
    ProgramYour sequenceSearches
    blastpproteinproteins
    blastnDNADNA
    blastxDNA, translated in all six reading framesproteins
    tblastnproteinDNA, translated in all six frames
    Protein searches find relatives much further back in evolution than DNA searches: when the sequence codes for a protein, compare proteins (blastp, or blastx from DNA).
  2. Database. For proteins: UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot (reviewed – a good first choice), the PDB (proteins with a known structure), AlphaFold DB, human UniProtKB, or all of UniProtKB (slow). For DNA: the coding sequences in ENA (human, mammals, mouse, all), or virus sequences.
  3. Sequence: FASTA, or just the letters.
  4. E-value cut-off and the number of Hits to keep.
  5. Your e-mail address: EBI asks for one with each job, and writes to it only if something goes wrong. Remember it in this browser keeps it here, on this computer only.
  6. Run. The job's state is shown while it waits and runs.

The examples (Try:) fill in the form – a myoglobin protein, a piece of human DNA; press Run after typing your e-mail address.

Reading the hits

ColumnMeans
E-valuehow many hits this good would turn up by chance in a database this size. The smaller, the better: below 1e-10 the similarity is almost certainly real (the sequences share an ancestor); around 0.01–1 it may be chance.
Bitsthe bit score: the quality of the alignment, independent of the database's size. Higher is better.

The genome browser

The genome browser (igv.js, the web edition of IGV) draws data along a genome: mapped reads, variants, genes, coverage. It is where you check, with your own eyes, what a program has claimed – a variant call, a deletion, a badly mapped region.

The genome

Choose it at the top left:

  • The project (it opens with this one) – a 1.5-Mb slice of chromosome 3 of GRCh38 around SCN5A, with the genes of the region, the exons of SCN5A, the two promoter variants and the haplotype block. Its chromosome is called chr3, with the positions of the whole chromosome, so the project's VCF and BED files show on it.
  • Hosted by the IGV team – whole genomes, with their genes: human hg38 and hg19, mouse, zebrafish, fly, worm, yeast. They need the internet.
  • A FASTA file of yours – any FASTA file in your home folder, once it has an index: samtools faidx genome.fa.

Another genome replaces the one shown, and the tracks you added to it go – they belong to that genome. If the new genome cannot be loaded (no internet, a server that does not answer), the one you had comes back with its tracks, and a message says why.

The chromosome names must match

A track is drawn only on a genome whose chromosome (sequence) names are the ones in the file. The project's files say chr1 and use GRCh38 positions: they show on the project's genome and on hg38, not on hg19. The practice reads in /data/examples/reads are on human_CYP2C19 and human_CYP2C9, two slices of GRCh37 – use their FASTA file as the genome (below).

Find your way

  • Type a position – chr3:38,648,000-38,653,000 – or, on the hosted genomes, a gene name (SCN5A) in the box at the top, and press Enter.
  • Drag the picture sideways to move; + and − (or a double-click) to zoom. Reads appear when you are zoomed in far enough.
  • Click a read, a variant or a gene for its details. The ⚙ of a track changes how it is drawn: colours, height, expanded or squished.

Reading the reads

  • The coverage track on top shows how many reads cover each base. Where many reads disagree with the reference, the bar is coloured by the bases they have.
  • Each bar is a read, coloured by its strand: pink forward, blue reverse. A coloured letter in a read is a base that differs from the reference; faint letters are bases of low quality.
  • A purple I is an insertion, a black line a deletion. Faint reads have mapping quality 0 (MAPQ 0): they fit equally well somewhere else in the genome.
  • A real heterozygous SNV shows the other base in about half the reads, on both strands; a sequencing error shows up in one or two reads, or on one strand only.

Variants

In a VCF track, each variant is a bar (its colour shows the frequency of the alternative allele); under it, each sample's genotype: dark blue heterozygous, cyan homozygous for the alternative allele, grey homozygous for the reference.

The IDE tab: RStudio and Spyder

Most of your work in the workbench will be done in notebooks, but some jobs are easier in an integrated development environment, or IDE. The IDE tab has two of them: RStudio for R and Spyder for Python. Both are imitations of the real programs, made for teaching, and they work in the same way as far as possible, so what you learn here carries over to RStudio and Spyder on your own computer.

A notebook or an IDE?

A notebook is the best place to explore your data. Each piece of code sits next to its results, and you can write notes in between, so the notebook becomes a record of what you did and what you found. An IDE is built for writing programs rather than records, and it is worth switching to one when:

  • your code has grown beyond a few cells, and you would rather keep it in a script that you can run again from the top whenever your data change;
  • you are writing a report in R Markdown or Quarto, or an interactive Shiny app (both in RStudio);
  • your code doesn’t do what you expected, and you want to go through it one line at a time and watch your variables change (Spyder’s debugger);
  • you want to see all of your variables at a glance, and look inside data frames and arrays as tables;
  • you will be using RStudio or Spyder on your own computer later, and want to get to know them first.

The two ways of working go well together. You can try something out in a notebook and then copy the code that works into a script, or write functions in a script and load them into a notebook with source("functions.R") in R or %run functions.py in Python.

Each IDE has its own R or Python

RStudio and Spyder each run their own copy of R or Python, separate from the one that the notebooks use, just as RStudio and Spyder start R and Python processes of their own on a computer. A variable that you create in a notebook therefore doesn’t exist in Spyder, and the other way round: if you need it in both places, run the code that creates it in both. What they do share are your files. A script in Spyder can read a table that a notebook wrote, and a notebook can open a plot that RStudio saved.

The first time you open an IDE, your browser has to download R or Python, which can take a minute or so (R alone is about 30 MB). After that it keeps a copy, so the IDE opens much more quickly on your next visit. An IDE that you have opened keeps running while you use the other tabs, and you can have both open at once: the buttons at the top of the IDE tab switch between them.

Opening your files in an IDE

  • In the Files tab, an R script or an R Markdown or Quarto document has an Open in RStudio button above it, and a Python script has Open in Spyder. You will find the same commands in the menu of a file (its ⋯ button, or a right-click).
  • In the Terminal, rstudio analysis.R and spyder script.py open files in the IDE, and rstudio or spyder on its own simply opens it.
  • Inside an IDE, use its own File ▸ Open (Ctrl+O) or its Files pane.

As in the real programs, a file that you edit in an IDE is saved only when you save it (Ctrl+S). RStudio shows the name of a file with unsaved changes in red, and Spyder adds an asterisk to it. If you try to leave or reload the page while a file has unsaved changes, your browser asks whether you really want to.

The AI assistant and the IDEs

When code fails in RStudio or Spyder, a ✦ Ask the AI assistant about this error button appears under the error. It sends the code and the error message to the assistant, together with the script you are working on, the end of the console, and the names and types of your variables (but not their values). If you go from the IDE tab to the assistant and ask a question of your own, the same information goes with it, unless you untick your code in the IDE under the message box. Code in the assistant’s answers can then go straight into the script that is open in RStudio or Spyder, with Put into RStudio or Put into Spyder. See The AI assistant for how to connect a model.

What is different from the real programs

Everything in the IDE tab runs inside your web browser, and that brings a few limits that RStudio and Spyder on a computer don’t have:

  • R and Python can’t start other programs, so system() in R and subprocess in Python don’t work. Instead, a line that starts with ! in Spyder’s console runs a command in the workbench’s terminal, and RStudio has a Terminal tab of its own next to the Console.
  • Spyder’s debugger and input(), and stopping code while it is running in either IDE, need the page to be cross-origin isolated. The workbench arranges this itself in most browsers (see when the debugger is not available).
  • Reports can be knitted to web pages (HTML), but not to PDF or Word, because the programs needed for those (pandoc and LaTeX) don’t run in a browser.
  • There are no projects, no version control (Git) and no plug-ins or add-ins.

Are the proteins of essential genes longer than the others?

When the debugger is not available

The debugger, input() and stopping code with Ctrl+C all need Python to pause and wait for you while it is in the middle of running something. A web page can only do that when it is cross-origin isolated. The workbench arranges this itself in most browsers, but not in every situation (in some private windows, for example). If the page is not isolated, the console says so when it starts. Everything else still works, but input() stops with an error instead of waiting for an answer, the debugger can’t be started, and the only way to stop code that runs for too long is Restart kernel. Reloading the page, or opening it in an ordinary window, usually helps; What is different in the browser explains more.

What is different from Spyder on a computer

  • The console’s Python runs inside your browser (Pyodide), and is separate from the Python of the notebooks.
  • Python can’t start threads or other processes, so threading, multiprocessing and subprocess don’t work; use !command for the programs of the terminal.
  • Figures are drawn in the Plots pane and the console only: there are no separate interactive windows, and desktop toolkits such as tkinter and Qt are not available.
  • There are no projects, no plug-ins and no version control.

The AI assistant answers questions about your code, an error message, a program or a method – and puts the code from its answers into a notebook or the terminal with one click. Its Agent tab goes further: it carries out a whole task in the terminal (see The AI agent).

Before you start
  • Never paste personal or patient data into an AI tool, here or anywhere else.
  • Check what it gives you. AI models make mistakes, invent options and references, and sound sure when they do. Run the code and look at the result.
  • Follow your course's rules on using AI in assessed work, and say where you used it.

Connect a model

The assistant uses an AI service of your choice, with a key of your own (or one you have been given). Your questions go from your browser straight to that service. Click Not connected or (AI settings), and choose the Service:

  • Google Gemini – make a free key with a Google account at aistudio.google.com/apikey (you must be 18 or over). On the free tier Google may use what you send to improve its products, and people may read it – except for users in the UK, the European Economic Area and Switzerland, whose free use Google treats like paid use (it keeps what is sent for a limited time, to detect misuse, and does not use it to improve its products). Each model allows a few requests a minute. When one model is busy, the page asks another, and the answer says which one replied.
  • Anthropic (Claude) – a key from the Claude Console, platform.claude.com (paid).
  • An OpenAI-compatible service – its address (base URL) and model: a service of your university, or a model running on your own computer, if it allows requests from web pages.

Paste the key, press Test, then Save. The model's name then shows at the top of the tab.

Where the key is kept

Only in this browser tab, in memory: reloading the page forgets it. Tick Keep the key until I close this browser tab to keep it across reloads. It is never written into your files, into downloads, or into the browser's long-term storage. Forget the key removes it. A key is like a password: do not share it, and do not paste it into notebooks or files.

Ask

Type your question and press Enter (Shift+Enter for a new line); Stop ends an answer. Under the box, choose what goes with your question:

  • the open notebook's code – its code cells, not their output, not your data;
  • your file names – names and sizes, not what is in the files;
  • your last commands – when you come from the Terminal, your last three commands and the end of what they printed;
  • your code in the IDE – when you come from the IDE tab: the script open in RStudio or Spyder (or the lines you have selected in it), the end of its console, and the names and types of its variables (not their values). A question from the IDE tab sends this instead of the open notebook’s code.

It also knows which programs the terminal has, and the versions of Python and R. From the other tabs: Ask AI on a notebook cell, or Ask the AI to explain this error under a cell that failed – this sends the cell's code with its error message (up to 2,500 characters), or with the beginning of its output (up to 1,200 characters); ✦ Ask the AI assistant about this error after a command that failed in the terminal – the command, its folder and the end of what it printed (up to 3,000 characters) – and under an error in RStudio or Spyder, which sends the code that failed (for a script that was run as a whole, the script), the error message and the IDE’s working directory, with your code in the IDE if that is ticked. The open notebook's code and your file names go with them if they are ticked under the box.

Without a key, common errors still get a short explanation from the page itself, and Copy a prompt for another AI tool copies your question, with its context, to paste into an AI tool you are allowed to use.

Use it well

  • Say what you want and what you have: the file and its format, the program, the error message as it is.
  • Ask for explanations, not only answers: explain each option of this command, why did this fail, what would go wrong if …. You learn more, and you notice its mistakes.
  • Check against the source: man samtools or samtools view --help in the terminal, help(function) in Python, ?function in R, and these help pages.
  • Look at the results, not only at the code: the number of reads, the first lines of a file, a plot. Code that runs is not code that is right.

The AI agent

Give the agent a whole task – map the example reads, call the variants and tell me how many there are. It carries it out in the Terminal, one command at a time, reads what each command prints before it decides the next, and reports. Every run is kept as a notebook.

Give it a task

  1. Connect a model first (see The AI assistant), then open the Agent tab.
  2. Type a task – or click one under Try a task – and Send.
  3. Watch: each step shows the command and what it printed, here and in the Terminal. Stop ends the task at any time.
  4. Read its report – then check it (below).

The agent works in a folder of its own, ~/ai-agent/run-1, run-2 …; programs put the files they make there. A further task goes on in the same folder, and the agent is told what was done before – until New starts a new folder.

What it may do

  • Run the commands of the terminal: the bioinformatics programs, the text tools, python and Rscript scripts, Snakemake. Not cd – every step starts in its folder, and it uses paths instead.
  • Read your files. What it changes outside its folder – a file it deletes or overwrites by mistake – is put back at the end of each step. What you change yourself while it works is yours, and stays.
  • Use the internet (curl, wget) only if you allow it: AI settings → Let the agent use the internet. Without it, curl and wget are refused and the agent is told not to use the network from Python or R – but a Python or R script could still reach the web, so read what it runs.

Limits: at most 20 steps for a task; a command that runs for more than 150 seconds is stopped; the model sees the last 3,000 characters of what each command prints. While the agent works, the terminal is its own: wait, or press Stop.

What is sent to the AI service

The task, the commands and everything they print – including what the agent reads from your files. Do not let it work on files with personal or patient data.

The record

Each run is written into its folder as a notebook, agent-record.ipynb: the task, each command as a %%bash cell with its output, and the report. Under the agent's card: Open the record (in the Notebook tab), .ipynb (download it), Folder (show it in Files). Run the notebook's cells again to see that the commands really make what the report says. The cells run in the notebook's own folder: to keep the agent's files as they are, copy the whole folder and run the copy of the record.

Check its work

  • Read the commands. Are the options what you would choose? The right reference, the right files, the right genome assembly?
  • Look at the results yourself: open the files, count, put the reads in the genome browser. The report can be wrong even when the commands are right – and a model may describe output it has not seen.
  • Could you do it without the agent? If not, ask the Chat tab to explain each step, and do it yourself in a notebook.

A table, in Python and in R

Are essential genes different? The example table describes 5,481 yeast genes – whether each is essential, how long its protein is, how many partners it has, how abundant it is. The same analysis in both languages: read the table, summarise it, plot it, and test what the plots suggest.

Each step has the Python code first, then the R code; each button takes the code of one language.

1. Read the table

import pandas as pd

One row per gene. essential is 1 when the yeast cannot live without the gene; interaction_partners counts the proteins its protein is known to interact with; human_homolog is 1 when the gene has a human counterpart.

2. Summarise by group

genes.groupby("essential")[["protein_length", "interaction_partners", "protein_abundance"]].median()
aggregate(cbind(protein_length, interaction_partners, protein_abundance) ~ essential,
          data = genes, FUN = median)

Medians, not means: these columns are skewed – a few proteins have thousands of partners. (R's formula drops the genes with a missing value in any of the columns.)

4. Test it

The Mann–Whitney (Wilcoxon rank-sum) test asks whether one group's values tend to be larger, without assuming a normal distribution.

from scipy.stats import mannwhitneyu

ess = genes.loc[genes.essential == 1, "interaction_partners"]
non = genes.loc[genes.essential == 0, "interaction_partners"]
mannwhitneyu(ess, non)
wilcox.test(interaction_partners ~ essential, data = genes)

Are genes with a human counterpart more often essential? Two yes/no columns: a table of counts and a chi-squared test.

from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency

counts = pd.crosstab(genes.human_homolog, genes.essential)
print(counts)
print(f"essential: {counts.loc[1, 1] / counts.loc[1].sum():.0%} with a human homologue, {counts.loc[0, 1] / counts.loc[0].sum():.0%} without")
chi2_contingency(counts)
counts <- table(homolog = genes$human_homolog, essential = genes$essential)
counts
prop.table(counts, margin = 1)
chisq.test(counts)

5. Two measurements together

Highly expressed genes use the codons that are read fastest (high codon bias). Spearman's correlation works on ranks, so it does not mind skewed values.

from scipy.stats import spearmanr

d = genes.dropna(subset=["codon_bias", "protein_abundance"])
print(spearmanr(d.codon_bias, d.protein_abundance))
d.plot.scatter(x="codon_bias", y="protein_abundance", logy=True, s=4, alpha=0.4, figsize=(5, 3.5))
plt.show()
cor.test(genes$codon_bias, genes$protein_abundance, method = "spearman", exact = FALSE)
ggplot(genes, aes(codon_bias, protein_abundance)) +
  geom_point(size = 0.6, alpha = 0.4) +
  scale_y_log10() +
  theme_bw()

Databases from code

The Databases tab asks one question at a time. Code asks a hundred – and keeps the record. This recipe shows the patterns: a request and its answer as a table, many at once, a polite loop, what to do when a service is busy, NCBI with Biopython, and the first reads of a file too big to download.

1. A request that tries again

Web services are shared and sometimes busy: now and then they answer 429 (too many requests) or 5xx (a problem of the server), or nothing at all. A small function waits and asks again – and stops with a clear error for anything else (a wrong identifier gives 400 or 404).

import time
import requests
import pandas as pd

def ask(method, url, tries=4, **kwargs):
    """Make a request; when the service is busy or does not answer, wait and try again."""
    for attempt in range(1, tries + 1):
        try:
            r = requests.request(method, url, **kwargs)
            if r.status_code not in (429, 500, 502, 503, 504):
                r.raise_for_status()          # 400, 404 …: a mistake in the request – stop
                return r
            problem = f"HTTP {r.status_code}"
        except requests.exceptions.ConnectionError:
            problem = "no answer"
        if attempt == tries:
            raise RuntimeError(f"{problem} from {url} after {tries} tries – try again later")
        print(f"{problem} – trying again in {2 ** attempt} s")
        time.sleep(2 ** attempt)

def get_json(url, params=None):
    return ask("GET", url, params=params, headers={"Accept": "application/json"}).json()

gene = get_json("https://rest.ensembl.org/lookup/symbol/homo_sapiens/CYP2C19")
gene["id"], gene["seq_region_name"], gene["start"], gene["end"]

2. Many at once

Many services take a list in one request (a POST request with the list in its body). One request for five genes is quicker – and kinder to the service – than five.

symbols = ["CYP2C19", "CYP2C9", "CYP2D6", "CYP3A4", "CYP1A2"]
r = ask("POST", "https://rest.ensembl.org/lookup/symbol/homo_sapiens", json={"symbols": symbols},
        headers={"Content-Type": "application/json", "Accept": "application/json"})
genes = pd.DataFrame([{"symbol": s, "ensembl": g["id"], "chromosome": g["seq_region_name"], "start": g["start"], "end": g["end"]}
                      for s, g in r.json().items()])
genes

3. A polite loop

When there is no batch request, loop – with a short pause between requests. Here: the reviewed UniProt entry of each gene.

rows = []
for s in symbols:
    data = get_json("https://rest.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/search",
                    params={"query": f"gene_exact:{s} AND organism_id:9606 AND reviewed:true",
                            "fields": "accession,length", "format": "json"})
    for e in data["results"]:
        rows.append({"symbol": s, "uniprot": e["primaryAccession"], "length": e["sequence"]["length"]})
    time.sleep(0.2)          # the service is shared
genes.merge(pd.DataFrame(rows), on="symbol")

Biopython's Bio.Entrez speaks NCBI's E-utilities: search, then fetch. NCBI asks who is asking – put your own address in Entrez.email.

6. The first reads of a big file

A sequencing run is usually far too big for the browser – the reads of SRR098401 are 7.5 GB per file. A Range request downloads only the start of a compressed file, and zlib unpacks as much as arrived:

import zlib

url = "https://ftp.sra.ebi.ac.uk/vol1/fastq/SRR098/SRR098401/SRR098401_1.fastq.gz"
r = ask("GET", url, headers={"Range": "bytes=0-4999999"})      # the first 5 MB
text = zlib.decompressobj(16 + zlib.MAX_WBITS).decompress(r.content).decode()
lines = text.split("\n")
keep = (len(lines) - 1) // 4 * 4                                # whole reads only
with open("SRR098401_start_1.fastq", "w") as fh:
    fh.write("\n".join(lines[:keep]) + "\n")
print(keep // 4, "reads")
!head -n 8 SRR098401_start_1.fastq

The first reads of a run come from the edge of the flow cell, and are often poor (all N): for a fair sample, use reads from the middle of the file, or trim and filter them first.

Which services work from the browser

A web page – and so a notebook here – can only ask services that allow requests from web pages (CORS): Ensembl, UniProt, NCBI's E-utilities, the EBI services (ENA, InterPro, QuickGO, BLAST …), RCSB PDB, AlphaFold DB, STRING, Reactome, Europe PMC, PubChem and the UCSC API do. Many others – KEGG, most web pages – do not: the request fails with no answer. Download such files with your browser and upload them (Your files).

What is different in the browser

Everything in the workbench runs on your own computer, inside this web page: the programs are the real ones, compiled to WebAssembly, and Python and R are the real languages. That brings some limits a server would not have.

Speed and memory

  • Each program runs on one processor core; options for threads (-t, -p, -@, -j, --threads, --cpu) are ignored, with a note. One program is different: PLINK 2 reads its files on threads of its own and computes on one or two (--threads 2 at most) – see programs on several threads. Programs run more slowly than on a server, and the speed depends on your computer: keep the data small.
  • A program, Python and R each have a few gigabytes of memory at most (WebAssembly is 32-bit: 4 GB is the ceiling, and browsers often allow less). Mapping reads to a whole human genome does not fit; regions, a few genes, bacterial and viral genomes do.
  • Python has no threads, no multiprocessing and no subprocess; input() does not work in a notebook, though it does in Spyder (in the IDE tab). R cannot run system(): use a %%bash cell.

Programs on several threads

PLINK 2 starts threads that share the program's memory. A browser allows that only in a page that is cross-origin isolated: the page and its workers come with two headers, Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy and Cross-Origin-Embedder-Policy, which also keep pictures of other sites out unless those sites allow them. Static hosting (GitHub Pages) cannot send the headers, so the workbench adds them itself with a small service worker (isolation-sw.js; it stores nothing): the first time you open the workbench, the page loads once more, at once. serve.py sends the headers itself.

  • If PLINK 2 says that the page is not isolated, reload the page. It cannot be isolated when it was not opened over https (or from localhost), or when the browser does not allow service workers there – some private windows do not.
  • The other programs, Python and R do not need isolation, and work either way. In the IDE tab, Spyder’s debugger and input(), and stopping code while it runs, need it too (see Spyder).
  • To turn isolation off – if a picture of another site does not show, say – open the workbench with ?isolation=off at the end of its address; open it without that to turn it on again.

Programs that are partly here

  • PLINK 2 is built without the LAPACK library, so --pca and --pheno-svd are not available (PLINK says so and stops). It works in 640 MiB of memory, the least it accepts: the terminal adds --threads 1 --memory 640 require unless you give them, and a larger --memory becomes 1024 (more threads than 2 become 2), with a note. --script and --rerun, which read options from a file, are not available: plink2 $(cat FILE) does the same. Its genotypes must fit in that memory – thousands of people and variants, not a whole biobank. It needs a cross-origin isolated page.

Files

  • Your files are kept in this browser on this computer, not on a server – see where your files are kept.
  • Browsers give a site a share of the free disk space – usually several gigabytes; My files shows how much is used. Files of hundreds of megabytes work, but slowly. Python and R are not given files over 300 MB, and a file over 1 GB is not kept in the browser at all: it is there while the page is open – download it if you need it.
  • Large intermediate files fill the space fast: pipe programs together (minimap2 … | samtools sort -o mapped.bam -) instead of writing a SAM file, and delete what you no longer need.

Installing

  • No sudo, apt or background jobs (&). Programs cannot be added to the terminal, and there is no need to: every program is installed already, so there is no conda here either. (To set up the same programs on another computer, see Keep and share your work.)
  • Python packages install with %pip install (pure-Python packages, and those Pyodide has built); R packages with install.packages() (those built for webR). Both last until the page is reloaded – except in RStudio, which remembers the R packages you installed there and fetches them again when library() needs them.
  • Some packages have no build for the browser yet – mostly those that need system libraries; the error says so.

The internet

  • Python is downloaded when a Python notebook is open, R when an R notebook is open – and the page opens the last notebook you had open when it starts (a Python one on the first visit) –, and again for Spyder or RStudio when you first open one of them in the IDE tab; packages when they are imported or installed: tens of megabytes, of which the browser keeps a copy for later visits.
  • A web page can only ask web services that allow requests from web pages (CORS). The big bioinformatics services do – Ensembl, UniProt, NCBI, EBI, the PDB, AlphaFold DB and the others of the Databases tab; KEGG and most web pages do not. This holds for notebooks, curl and wget alike.
  • R in the browser can only make GET requests; use Python for POST requests.
  • The Databases tab, the AI assistant, the hosted genomes of the genome browser and downloads from the PDB need the internet all the time; the project's own data come with the site.

The tabs

  • Snakemake is a browser edition of its commonly used core: rules, wildcards, expand, -n, --dag, --summary; no cluster execution.
  • The IDE tab: RStudio and Spyder are imitations, made for teaching, of the programs of the same names. Each has an R or a Python of its own, separate from the notebooks’. Reports are knitted to HTML only (pandoc and LaTeX, which PDF and Word need, do not run in a browser); Shiny apps need the page’s service worker; there are no projects, no Git and no plug-ins (The IDE tab).
  • The 3D viewer needs WebGL (3D graphics in the browser): if it does not start, turn on hardware acceleration in the browser's settings.
  • The workbench is made for a laptop or desktop screen; on a phone or tablet it works, cramped.

Use a current Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari.

When something goes wrong

The usual problems, and what to do about them. Error messages in the notebook and the terminal often carry a hint of their own – and Ask the AI assistant about this error explains them (AI assistant).

Starting

Python or R takes long to start
The first start of a visit downloads the language (tens of megabytes); later visits use the browser's copy and are quicker. The label at the top right of a notebook says what it is doing.
A tab says it could not start
Reload the page (your files are kept). For the 3D viewer: turn on hardware acceleration in the browser's settings. Use a current Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari.
The page is slow or stops answering
A long computation is running: Stop it (notebook), or press Ctrl+C in the terminal – once, and what follows the running program is skipped; twice, and the program itself is stopped. Close other heavy tabs of the browser.

Files

My files are gone
They are kept in one browser on one computer – is it the same browser, the same computer, the same web address, and not a private window? If the browser's data for the site was cleared, they cannot be recovered: download what you want to keep (Keep and share your work).
"No such file or directory"
The file is not where the code looked. A notebook works in its own folder (~/notebooks): use full paths such as /data/examples/… or /home/user/… (Python's open() does not understand ~). %pwd or getwd() shows the folder; the Files tab shows the names.
"Read-only file system" or "Permission denied"
/data/examples is read-only: its files cannot be changed, and nothing new can be written into it – not even what a program writes next to its input (samtools faidx writes its index next to the FASTA file). Copy what you need into your home folder and work there.
A PDF "cannot be read as a PDF"
The Files tab draws PDF files itself, so no PDF plug-in of the browser is needed. A file it cannot read is damaged, is not a PDF, or is not finished: a plot that R or Python is still drawing into is written only when it is closed – dev.off() after pdf("plot.pdf") in R, plt.savefig("plot.pdf") or the end of with PdfPages(…) in Python. Download the file to open it with another program.
The space is running out
My files (top right) shows how much is used. Delete large intermediate files – SAM files, unsorted BAM files, trimmed reads – or download and delete them.

Programs and code

"command not found"
The program is not in the workbench: help lists what is. Python packages: %pip install NAME; R packages: install.packages("NAME").
plink2: "… this page is not" cross-origin isolated
PLINK 2 works on several threads, which a browser allows only in a cross-origin isolated page. Reload the page: the workbench isolates it as it starts. It cannot when the page was not opened over https (or from localhost), or in a browser window that allows no service workers (some private windows) – see programs on several threads.
A variant caller says an index is missing, or that the chromosomes do not match
Index the BAM files (samtools index) and the reference (samtools faidx) – again after a program has written a new BAM file. The reference must be the one the reads were mapped to, with the sequence names of the BAM header (samtools view -H file.bam | grep @SQ). See Genome browser.
hmmscan: "Failed to open binary auxfiles … use hmmpress first"
hmmscan searches a collection of profiles that hmmpress has prepared: run hmmpress FILE.hmm first, in a folder of your own – it writes four files next to the profiles, which it cannot do in /data/examples. When hmmpress says "Delete old hmmpress indices first", the collection is pressed already; after changing its profiles, press it again with hmmpress -f FILE.hmm.
hmmsearch or hmmscan: "File format problem in trying to open HMM file"
The file given as the profile is not one – an alignment, or sequences. Build the profile from the alignment first (hmmbuild NEW.hmm ALIGNMENT.sto), and mind the order: the profiles come first and the sequences second, for hmmsearch and hmmscan alike.
plink2: "--pca requires plink2 to be built with LAPACK"
This build of PLINK 2 has no LAPACK, the library its principal components (--pca) and --pheno-svd need: they are not available here. The rest of PLINK works. See Genotypes and association.
plink2 --glm: "invoked without --covar … usually an analytical mistake"
PLINK asks for covariates in an association test – ancestry (principal components), sex, age – because a test without them is usually confounded. Give them with --covar FILE, or, when you are sure, add the modifier: --glm allow-no-covars.
plink2: "Failed to open NAME.psam" (or .pgen, .pvar, .bed …)
--pfile NAME reads three files, NAME.pgen, NAME.pvar and NAME.psam (--bfile NAME: .bed, .bim, .fam): give the name without its ending, and make the files first (plink2 --vcf FILE.vcf.gz --make-pgen --out NAME).
A program says it is out of memory, or stops without a message
The data is too big for the browser: take a part of it (samtools view of a region, seqtk sample) – see What is different in the browser.
Python or R forgot my variables
The language was restarted – by Restart, Stop, or a reload of the page. Run the notebook's cells again from the top (Run all).
The terminal does not let me type
The AI agent is working in it: wait, or press Stop in the AI assistant tab.

The internet

"Failed to fetch", "no answer", a network error
Either the internet connection is off, or the site does not allow requests from web pages (CORS – KEGG and most web pages do not). Download such files with your browser and upload them in the Files tab.
HTTP 429, "too many requests"
Wait a minute; in code, pause between requests (Databases from code).
HTTP 400 or 404
The service did not understand the query, or there is no such identifier: check the spelling and the version (NM_000769.4).
HTTP 500 or 503
The service is busy or down – try again later.

The other tabs

The genome browser shows nothing
Is the right genome chosen? The chromosome names of the file must be those of the genome (human_CYP2C19 for the example genome, chr10 for hg19/hg38). A BAM file needs its .bai (samtools index), a FASTA genome its .fai (samtools faidx). Reads appear only when you zoom in.
RStudio or Spyder takes a long time to open, or says it could not be opened
The first time, the IDE downloads R or Python, which can take a minute or so on a slow connection. If it fails, check your internet connection and press Try again, or reload the page; your files are kept.
My variable is missing in RStudio or Spyder (or in the notebook)
Each IDE has an R or a Python of its own, separate from the notebooks’, so a variable only exists where you created it. Run the code that creates it in the IDE as well (The IDE tab).
Spyder: input() or the debugger doesn’t work
Both need the page to be cross-origin isolated, and the console says so when it starts if it isn’t. Reload the page, or open it in an ordinary (not private) window (Spyder).
RStudio: "there is no package called …"
In RStudio, as on your own computer, a package has to be installed once before library() can load it: run install.packages("NAME"), or use Tools ▸ Install Packages… (RStudio).
The AI assistant says "Not connected", the key is not accepted, or it is busy
Check the service and key in AI settings and press Test. Free keys allow only a few requests per minute: wait a minute.

Reporting a problem

Say which browser and computer you use, what you did, and what happened – with the exact message (copy it, or take a screenshot). Leave out any personal data.

Your data and privacy

The workbench has no server of its own that keeps or sees your work: it runs in your browser. What leaves your computer goes to the public services you use, and only when you use them.

What is kept on your computer

  • Your files – notebooks, results, uploads, your logbook – in this browser's storage for the site (IndexedDB).
  • In the browser's local storage: your last 200 terminal commands; the boxes you ticked in the guide's checklists; settings – the open tab and notebook, the help page, the genome, the choices of the AI assistant (not its key), your name in the workbench if you put one in, and your e-mail address for BLAST if you ask to remember it.
  • The AI key: only in the tab's memory, or, if you tick it, until the tab is closed (session storage). Never in a file or in long-term storage.

On a shared computer, the next person who uses the same browser account can see what is stored: download your work, then My files → Start afresh. That deletes your files, the terminal's commands and the remembered e-mail address; your name in the workbench and the choices of the AI assistant and of the help pane stay, and the AI key is forgotten when you close the tab. The workbench itself sets no cookies and has no tracking or analytics.
