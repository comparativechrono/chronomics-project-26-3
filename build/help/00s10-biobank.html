Does the haplotype do anything to the heart? Two biobank-scale datasets can answer: a genome-wide study of a heart-rhythm disease, and a catalogue of how variants change gene expression. Look up your variants in both, and put the pieces together into a mechanism – carefully.

You will learn
  • to read GWAS summary statistics: effect allele, effect size, P-value
  • to look a variant up in a disease study and judge its signal in context
  • eQTLs: genetic effects on gene expression (GTEx)
  • to assemble evidence into a mechanism, and to say what is still open

1. The haplotype and Brugada syndrome

SCN5A loss of function causes Brugada syndrome. Barc et al. (2022) ran a genome-wide association study of it; you have the SCN5A–SCN10A locus of their results. Look up your tags:

cd ~/project
head -1 /data/examples/scn5a/gwas/brugada_Barc2022_SCN5A_SCN10A.tsv | tr '\t' '\n' | head -8 | cat -n
grep -wE "rs41310749|rs41310239" /data/examples/scn5a/gwas/brugada_Barc2022_SCN5A_SCN10A.tsv \
  | awk -F'\t' '{print $2, "pos="$4, $5"/"$6, "beta="$7, "p="$20}'
Check

Both are genome-wide significant: rs41310749 has effect (beta) ≈ 0.287 for its A allele, P ≈ 1.1 × 10−10; rs41310239 is almost identical. A beta of 0.287 on the log-odds scale is an odds ratio of e0.287 ≈ 1.33 – each copy of the derived allele raises Brugada risk by about a third. The derived haplotype you have tracked all project is a risk haplotype.

2. But whose signal is it?

How strong is your haplotype compared with the rest of the locus? Find the most significant variant in the file:

awk -F'\t' 'NR>1 && $20!="NA" {print $20, $4, $2}' /data/examples/scn5a/gwas/brugada_Barc2022_SCN5A_SCN10A.tsv \
  | sort -g | head -5
Check

The strongest signal is far stronger – P around 10−150, up near chr3:38,71x,xxx, at the SCN10A end of the locus (the famous conduction enhancer region of stage 7). Your promoter haplotype, at P ≈ 10−10, is a real but minor part of a vast association signal.

A signal points to a haplotype, not a letter

A GWAS hit marks a stretch of DNA, not a single culprit: every variant in LD with the true cause lights up together. Your promoter haplotype is associated with Brugada syndrome – but so is a much larger region around it, led from the SCN10A end. To know whether the promoter haplotype matters on its own, you would have to hold the stronger signal fixed and ask whether the promoter still adds anything – a conditional analysis, which needs the individual-level data, not just these summary numbers. That is the honest limit of what you can conclude here, and a key point for your discussion.

3. Does the haplotype change how much SCN5A is made?

A promoter's job is to set a gene's output, so ask directly: is the variant an eQTL for SCN5A? GTEx has the answer. Query it live for the heart:

curl -s "https://gtexportal.org/api/v2/association/dyneqtl?gencodeId=ENSG00000183873.15&variantId=chr3_38651526_G_A_b38&tissueSiteDetailId=Heart_Atrial_Appendage&datasetId=gtex_v8" \
  | jq '{tissue:"Heart_Atrial_Appendage", nes, pValue}'

And the pre-computed table across all tissues is in the data:

e <- read.delim("/data/examples/scn5a/gtex/GTEx_v8_SCN5A_rs41310749.tsv")
e <- e[e$gene == "SCN5A", ]
e[order(e$p_value), ][1:6, c("tissue", "NES_altA", "p_value")]
Check

rs41310749 is a significant eQTL for SCN5A: the derived A allele lowers SCN5A expression – strongly in whole blood (NES ≈ −0.35, P ≈ 10−10) and fibroblasts, and in the same direction in the heart (atrial appendage NES ≈ −0.10, P ≈ 0.03; left ventricle similar but weaker). It does not affect SCN10A.

4. Put it together

A coherent thread

The derived promoter haplotype lowers SCN5A expression (eQTL) → less Nav1.5 → the kind of reduced sodium current that causes Brugada syndrome – and the haplotype is indeed associated with higher Brugada risk (GWAS). Three independent lines – a motif that is weakened (stage 8), lower expression, higher disease risk – point the same way. That is a satisfying, testable story. But remember the caveats: the eQTL is clearest outside the heart, the disease signal is dominated by the SCN10A end, and none of this proves the promoter haplotype acts on its own. A good scientist holds the mechanism and its limits in mind at once.
