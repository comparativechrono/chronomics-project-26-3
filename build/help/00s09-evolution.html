Stage 9 · Evolution: where did the haplotype come from?

Weeks 9–10about 7 hours

A haplotype has a history. Is it old or young? Did it pass to us from Neanderthals? Are the variant sites, and the clock E-boxes around them, the kind of DNA evolution has kept untouched? Line up the SCN5A promoter across ten primates, read the archaic human genomes, and find out.

You will learn
  • ancestral and derived alleles, read from other species
  • multiple sequence alignment with kalign
  • to read archaic (Neanderthal, Denisovan) genotypes
  • sequence conservation, and what it suggests about function

1. Line up the promoter across primates

The file scn5a_promoter_primates.fa holds the SCN5A promoter of ten primates. Align them with kalign:

cd ~/project
cp /data/examples/scn5a/primates/scn5a_promoter_primates.fa .
grep -c ">" scn5a_promoter_primates.fa
kalign -i scn5a_promoter_primates.fa -o results/promoter_aln.fa --type dna --format fasta
grep ">" results/promoter_aln.fa

Open the alignment in the Files tab (it has an alignment viewer) and scroll through it. Most columns are the same letter in every species – the promoter is well conserved.

Check

Ten species align (human, chimpanzee, bonobo, gorilla, orangutan, gibbon, two macaques, vervet, mouse lemur), into an alignment a little wider than the 2.2 kb human sequence (gaps are inserted to make the columns line up).

2. What was there before? Ancestral alleles

The allele our ancestors carried is usually the one other primates still have. Read the aligned column at each variant. The Add to notebook button opens R; this short function finds the column of a human position in the alignment and prints every species' base:

aln <- local({
  L <- readLines("~/project/results/promoter_aln.fa"); s <- list(); nm <- NULL
  for (ln in L) if (startsWith(ln, ">")) { nm <- sub("^>", "", ln); s[[nm]] <- "" } else s[[nm]] <- paste0(s[[nm]], ln)
  s
})
START <- 38649600                         # the first base of the human promoter sequence
col_of <- function(gpos) { g <- START; h <- strsplit(aln[["human"]], "")[[1]]
  for (i in seq_along(h)) { if (h[i] != "-") { if (g == gpos) return(i); g <- g + 1 } } }
base_at <- function(gpos) sapply(aln, function(s) substr(s, col_of(gpos), col_of(gpos)))
base_at(38651526)      # rs41310749
base_at(38650537)      # rs41310239
Check

At rs41310749 every primate has G – the human reference allele. So G is ancestral and the A allele of your haplotype is the newer, derived one, at a site otherwise unchanged across millions of years. At rs41310239 the picture is mixed: human, chimpanzee and bonobo have A, but gorilla, orangutan and gibbon have G, and the monkeys are hard to line up here at all (dashes in the alignment) – this is the repetitive, GC-rich patch from stage 2, where even the aligner struggles. The site has changed more than once in primates.

3. Did it come from Neanderthals?

Modern humans carry stretches of DNA inherited from Neanderthals and Denisovans. Did your haplotype? We extracted the archaic genotypes at the two sites for you (the method is in data-build/); read them:

column -t /data/examples/scn5a/primates/archaic_alleles.tsv 2>/dev/null || cat /data/examples/scn5a/primates/archaic_alleles.tsv
Check

The Denisovan is homozygous for the ancestral alleles at both sites (G/G, A/A); the Vindija Neanderthal is G/G at rs41310749 (the other calls are missing). None of the archaic genomes carries a derived allele. So the haplotype is not inherited from archaic humans – it arose in modern humans after the split, consistent with the common, young-looking allele you have been tracking.

4. Are the E-boxes conserved?

In stage 2 you found three canonical E-boxes in the promoter. Conserved DNA is more likely to be functional – so are the E-boxes kept across primates? For each, count how many of the nine other primates have the identical six letters:

ebox_cons <- function(gpos) {
  c0 <- col_of(gpos); hum6 <- gsub("-", "", substr(aln[["human"]], c0, c0 + 7))
  others <- setdiff(names(aln), "human")
  sum(sapply(others, function(sp) startsWith(gsub("-", "", substr(aln[[sp]], c0, c0 + 7)), substr(hum6,1,6))))
}
sapply(c(`-147` = 38649834, `-446` = 38650133, `-1748` = 38651435), ebox_cons)
Check

The two E-boxes nearest the gene (at −147 and −446 from the TSS) are conserved in almost every primate – the kind of deep conservation that marks a working switch. The E-box at −1748, the one closest to rs41310749, is much less conserved. An interesting contrast to think about: the clock motifs right at the promoter are kept, while the one by your variant is not.

Think
  • rs41310749's site is unchanged across all primates, yet carries a common human variant. What does that combination suggest – is the site under strong constraint, or not?
  • The haplotype is absent from Neanderthals and Denisovans and is young. Does "young and common" fit better with drift or with selection? What would you need to test selection properly?
  • Why might the proximal E-boxes be conserved but the one by the variant not? What does that say about which motifs matter?
