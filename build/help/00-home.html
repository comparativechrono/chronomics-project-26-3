One promoter, many genomes

Week 1about 3 hours

A research project of the Chronomic Medicine Group. You will take two small variants in the promoter of a heart gene, SCN5A, and ask what larger stretch of DNA they belong to – reading hundreds of complete human genomes, scoring the motifs the variants sit in, tracing them back through our evolution, and testing them in biobank-scale data on the heart.

The question

SCN5A makes Nav1.5, the sodium channel that starts each heartbeat. Its promoter – the switch that controls how much of the channel is made – carries two variants that our group has studied for years for a link to the circadian (daily) control of the heart: rs41310749 and rs41310239. They are known to travel together. What nobody has described is the larger haplotype – the whole block of DNA inherited as one piece – that they are part of.

Your project: use the long-read genomes of the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium and the 1000 Genomes Project to find and measure that block; work out which transcription-factor motifs the variants change (including the clock's E-boxes); trace the variants and the motifs through archaic humans and other primates; and ask what the block does, in a genome-wide study of a heart-rhythm disease and in gene-expression data.

As far as we know, nobody has described this haplotype before. The guide takes you through it in eleven stages, about one a week. Each stage teaches you the tools it needs – you do not need to have programmed before. By the end you will have used a Linux terminal, real bioinformatics programs, R, a genome browser and public databases, and you will have results of your own to write up.

The plan

Stages 1–7 are the core of the project. Stages 8, 9 and 10 each add a separate line of evidence, and every stage ends with going further – ideas to follow if you have time, or if you want to make the project your own. The guide is written for a year: take the stages slowly, read around them, and bring your questions to your supervisor.

How this page works

  • The guide is on the left; the workbench is on the right. The tabs along the top of the guide are the stages; Help is the manual of the tools. Drag the line between the two halves to make either wider.
  • Grey boxes with code have a button: Run in the terminal (terminal commands) or Add to notebook (R code). The buttons save typing, but type the first few commands of each stage yourself – that is how they stick.
  • Checklists like the one below have boxes to tick. What you tick is saved in this browser, and the tab of the stage fills in as you go.
  • Hints and answers hide behind Show a hint. Try first; looking is fine when you are stuck.
  • Search (top of this pane) finds any word in the guide and in the help.
  • New words are explained in the Glossary; the data are described in The project data, and the papers in The reading list.
Your files live in this browser

Everything you make is kept in this browser on this computer – not on a server. Use the same computer and browser each time, and once a week click My files → Download all my files (top right) to keep a copy. In a private window, or after clearing the browser's data, your files are gone.

Working on your own

  • Keep a logbook. Every research project has one. Yours is a text file, ~/project/logbook.md: what you did, what you found, what you did not understand. Bring your questions to your supervisor.
  • One stage a week is about right, with time to read. Some take longer – that is normal.
  • When you are stuck: read the error message (it usually says what is wrong); check the hint; search the guide; ask the AI tab (it needs a key of your own – see The AI assistant – and it can be wrong, so check what it says); write the question in your logbook for your supervisor.
  • Check yourself. The green Check boxes say what you should see. If your numbers differ, go back a step – a small slip early on changes everything after it.

Ground rules

  • All the data here are public and come from people who agreed to their genomes being shared openly, or are summary numbers with no individual in them. Never put personal or patient data into this workbench or into an AI tool.
  • Cite the data and the programs you use (The project data has the papers). If you use the AI assistant, say so in your report, and how.
  • Your results are yours: the guide shows you how to get them, and the checks tell you when you have; what they mean is for you to think about and discuss.

Before stage 1
