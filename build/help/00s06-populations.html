Stage 6 · The block across populations

Weeks 6–7about 5 hours

A haplotype is not the same everywhere. Measure how common the two-SNP haplotype is in each of the 1000 Genomes Project's five continental groups, and you will find a striking pattern – and a clue to the haplotype's history.

You will learn
  • allele and haplotype frequencies across populations
  • joining a genotype table to a table of who the people are
  • why LD and block structure differ between populations
  • what frequency differences can (and cannot) tell you about a variant's history

1. The haplotype in everyone

This time use all 3,202 people (more data for frequencies). Extract the two variants for everyone, and read the sample table that says which population each belongs to:

cd ~/project
bcftools view -m2 -M2 -v snps -r chr3:38650537,chr3:38651526 \
  /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/1kgp_scn5a_2Mb.vcf.gz \
  | bcftools query -f '%POS\t%REF\t%ALT[\t%GT]\n' | sed 's/|/\t/g' > results/kgp_two_all.tsv
bcftools query -l /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/1kgp_scn5a_2Mb.vcf.gz > results/kgp_samples_order.txt
head -3 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/1kgp_samples.tsv
kv <- read.delim("~/project/results/kgp_two_all.tsv", header = FALSE, na.strings = ".")
samples <- readLines("~/project/results/kgp_samples_order.txt")
hap_names <- paste0(rep(samples, each = 2), "_hap", 1:2)
G <- as.matrix(kv[, -(1:3)])
a <- G[kv$V1 == 38651526, ]; b <- G[kv$V1 == 38650537, ]
both <- as.integer(a == 1 & b == 1)
# which superpopulation each haplotype belongs to
samp <- read.delim("/data/examples/scn5a/1000g/1kgp_samples.tsv")
sup <- samp$superpopulation[match(sub("_hap[12]$", "", hap_names), samp$sample)]
round(tapply(both, sup, mean) * 100, 1)       # haplotype frequency (%) per group
Check

The two-SNP haplotype is common in Europeans (EUR ≈ 16.8%), South Asians (SAS ≈ 18.9%) and the Americas (AMR ≈ 13.3%), less common in East Asians (EAS ≈ 6.8%), and rare in Africans (AFR ≈ 1.5%).

2. The African exception

Why so rare in Africa? Look at the two variants separately there:

afr <- sup == "AFR"
round(c(rs41310749_A = mean(a[afr], na.rm = TRUE),
        rs41310239_G = mean(b[afr], na.rm = TRUE)), 4)
table(rs41310749_A = a[afr], rs41310239_G = b[afr])
Check

In Africans rs41310749-A is still reasonably common (~13%), but rs41310239-G is rare (~1.5%) – and almost every rs41310239-G chromosome also carries rs41310749-A. So one allele sits entirely on the other's background (D′ near 1), but because their frequencies are so different, r² between them is low (~0.1). The two-SNP haplotype needs both minor alleles, so it follows the rarer one.

Why LD differs between populations

African populations are the oldest, and have had the most generations for recombination to break haplotypes apart; populations that expanded out of Africa passed through bottlenecks that left longer, commoner shared haplotypes. So a block that is long and frequent in Europeans can be short or rare in Africans. This is why you defined the block within a population in stage 5 – and why "the block" is really "the block in this group".

Think
  • The haplotype is commonest in South Asians and Europeans and rare in Africans. Does that pattern suggest the derived alleles arose before or after the migrations out of Africa? What else could produce it (drift, selection)?
  • Does a frequency difference between populations tell you anything about the haplotype's effect on the heart? (Be careful here – this is a common error to discuss in your report.)
  • If you tested the block's LD within Africans, would you expect it to be wider or narrower than in Europeans? (You can check.)
