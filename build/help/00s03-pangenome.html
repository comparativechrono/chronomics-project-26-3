Stage 3 · From a pangenome to haplotypes

Weeks 3–4about 6 hours

To see what haplotype the two variants belong to, you need complete genomes read well enough to be sure of every base – even in the awkward, repetitive promoter. That is what a pangenome gives you. Learn to read a VCF file, handle the messy records that a repetitive region produces, and pull out the two variants in 462 long-read human haplotypes.

You will learn
  • what a pangenome is, and why long reads matter in a region like this one
  • VCF files: records, genotypes, and phasing (0|1)
  • bcftools view and query; giving variants their rsIDs with annotate
  • splitting multiallelic records (norm -m-any) and keeping SNPs (-v snps)
What a pangenome is

For twenty years "the human genome" meant one reference sequence, and everyone else's reads were lined up against it. A pangenome is built from many complete genomes at once – here, the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium (HPRC) release 2, assembled from long reads for 231 people. Because every genome is assembled in full, the pangenome reads straight through repetitive, GC-rich stretches that short reads stumble over – exactly the kind of sequence you found around rs41310239 in stage 2. Each person contributes two assembled chromosomes, so 231 people give 462 haplotypes.

1. Look at the pangenome's view of the promoter

The file hprc_scn5a_promoter.vcf.gz holds the pangenome's "bubbles" – places where the genomes differ – across the promoter, as whole alleles. Look at a few:

cd ~/project
bcftools view -H /data/examples/scn5a/hprc/hprc_scn5a_promoter.vcf.gz | cut -f1-5 | head
bcftools query -l /data/examples/scn5a/hprc/hprc_scn5a_2Mb.vcf.gz | wc -l

view -H prints the records without the header; cut -f1-5 keeps the position and alleles; query -l lists the samples. Notice that at and around the variants the alleles are not always single letters – the promoter is a tangle of overlapping differences.

Check

There are 231 samples. Among the promoter records you will see the position 38,650,53x represented by more than two alleles (a single-letter change and small insertions) – a multiallelic record. That is the repetitive sequence from stage 2 making itself felt.

2. Give the variants their names, and tidy the records

The pangenome file has no rsIDs. dbSNP does: bcftools annotate copies the rsID onto any record with the same position and alleles. Then split the multiallelic records into one allele each (norm -m-any) and keep only the SNPs (-v snps):

bcftools annotate -a /data/examples/scn5a/reference/dbsnp157_scn5a_2Mb.vcf.gz -c ID \
  -Oz -o results/hprc_rsid.vcf.gz /data/examples/scn5a/hprc/hprc_scn5a_2Mb.vcf.gz
tabix -f -p vcf results/hprc_rsid.vcf.gz
bcftools norm -m-any results/hprc_rsid.vcf.gz -Oz -o results/hprc_split.vcf.gz
tabix -f -p vcf results/hprc_split.vcf.gz
bcftools view -H -v snps -r chr3:38650537,chr3:38651526 results/hprc_split.vcf.gz | cut -f1-5
Check

You get the two clean SNPs, now named: chr3 38650537 rs41310239 A G and chr3 38651526 rs41310749 G A. (Without -v snps you would also see an insertion at 38,650,537 that dbSNP gives the same rsID – a reminder to check the alleles, not just the name.)

3. Genotypes and phasing

Each person has a genotype at each variant. Look at the first few, for rs41310749:

bcftools view -v snps -r chr3:38651526 results/hprc_split.vcf.gz | bcftools query -f '[%SAMPLE=%GT ]\n' | tr ' ' '\n' | head

A genotype like 0|1 means: the reference allele on one chromosome, the alternate on the other. The bar | (not a slash /) means the data are phased – you know which allele is on which chromosome. You need phasing to read haplotypes, and the pangenome gives it to you for free, because each chromosome was assembled on its own.

4. Make a table of the two variants' genotypes

Write the phased genotypes of both variants to a table, one row per variant, splitting each 0|1 into its two haplotypes:

bcftools view -v snps -r chr3:38650537,chr3:38651526 results/hprc_split.vcf.gz \
  | bcftools query -f '%POS\t%ID\t%REF\t%ALT[\t%GT]\n' | sed 's/|/\t/g' > results/hprc_two_variants.tsv
bcftools query -l results/hprc_split.vcf.gz > results/hprc_samples_order.txt
awk '{print $1, $2, "columns:", NF}' results/hprc_two_variants.tsv

[ ... ] in the query is repeated for every sample; sed 's/|/\t/g' turns each 0|1 into two columns, so the table has 4 + 462 columns: position, rsID, the two alleles, then 0 or 1 for each of the 462 haplotypes.

Check

Two rows, each with 466 columns (4 + 462). You will turn this table into haplotypes in stage 4.

Think
  • Why can you read haplotypes straight from this file, but not from an ordinary short-read VCF without a separate "phasing" step?
  • The pangenome has 462 haplotypes; the 1000 Genomes Project (stage 5) has 6,404. What does each give you that the other does not? (Hint: long reads vs many people.)
